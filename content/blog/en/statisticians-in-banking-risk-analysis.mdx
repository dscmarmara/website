Statistics graduates have a wide range of career opportunities across data-driven industries, from finance to technology, thanks to their skills in analytical thinking, modeling, and data analysis. One of these industries is banking. At its core, banking is not just about managing money; it is also about anticipating uncertainty and potential threats and managing them before they turn into losses. This is where the Risk Analytics function comes in.

Risk Analytics is responsible for measuring the financial uncertainties a bank may face in the course of its activities using mathematical and statistical methods. Its role is to assess risks under normal conditions while also helping the bank maintain a safe operating position under highly adverse but plausible scenarios—often referred to as *stress scenarios*—involving macroeconomic variables such as exchange rates, inflation, and interest rates.

Although risk analytics in banking covers a broad range of areas, three of the key areas where statistics and data modeling are used most intensively are **Credit Risk, Liquidity Risk, and Market Risk**.

### Credit Risk

Credit risk is the possibility that a bank will suffer a loss because a person or institution fails to repay its debt on time or in full. By analyzing customers' historical financial data, spending and payment behavior, and demographic characteristics, banks develop credit scoring models and scorecards.

Logistic Regression and Machine Learning algorithms are used to estimate key credit risk parameters such as **Probability of Default (PD)**, **Loss Given Default (LGD)**, and **Exposure at Default (EAD)**. These analyses help determine who should receive credit, how much credit should be allocated, and how much the bank should set aside to cover potential credit losses.

However, the credit process does not end once a loan is approved. Continuously monitoring a customer's financial position and payment behavior throughout the repayment period is equally critical.

This is where an **Early Warning System (EWS)** comes into play. EWS continuously monitors account activity and overdue payments using statistical models. The goal is to identify early signs of financial stress before a borrower actually defaults, helping the bank reduce potential losses and keep its risk management framework responsive and dynamic.

### Liquidity Risk

Liquidity risk is the risk that a bank may be unable to meet its financial obligations when they become due because it cannot access sufficient cash when needed. One of the most important indicators of liquidity risk is the **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)**.

Made mandatory following the 2008 global financial crisis, the LCR measures a bank's ability to cover its net cash outflows over a 30-day period under a severe stress scenario using sufficient high-quality liquid assets.

Historical deposit movements, loan repayments, and customer behavior are analyzed to generate cash flow forecasts for different periods. In addition, the bank's liquidity position is tested under stress scenarios such as customers withdrawing deposits faster than expected or funding becoming less available in financial markets.

The bank's ability to cover potential net cash outflows during periods of stress with the cash and high-quality liquid assets it holds is periodically assessed using statistical models, helping ensure that the bank remains above the applicable regulatory thresholds.

### Market Risk

Market risk is the possibility that investments or portfolios may lose value as a result of price fluctuations in financial markets or changes in interest rates and exchange rates.

There are four main types of market risk: **Bond Risk, Interest Rate Risk, Foreign Exchange Risk, and Commodity Price Risk**.

Time Series Models, Monte Carlo simulations, **Value at Risk (VaR)**, and **Expected Shortfall (ES)** analyses are used to assess different scenarios and contribute to ensuring that the bank maintains sufficient capital to withstand potential losses.

Risk models are never simply built and left to run on their own. The accuracy of forecasts and risk metrics is continuously assessed through weekly and monthly backtesting. Model errors and deviations are regularly reviewed, and the relevant metrics are periodically updated in response to changing market conditions.

As part of this process, forecasts are developed for macroeconomic indicators such as inflation, interest rates, and exchange rates, while the risks associated with trading portfolios are also assessed on a regular basis.

### The Tools Behind Risk Analytics

SQL, Python, R, Power BI, Tableau, and Excel are among the key data processing and analytical tools used to put these analyses, simulations, and models into practice.

**SQL** is used to query raw data from databases containing millions of records, clean it, and prepare it for modeling. **Python and R** are particularly useful for more advanced analysis, including statistical testing, machine learning algorithms, time series modeling, and data manipulation with tools such as NumPy, pandas, SciPy, and scikit-learn.

**Power BI and Tableau** are used to build dashboards that visualize calculated risk metrics, stress test results, and portfolio distributions and present them to relevant teams and management.

**Excel** continues to serve as a supporting tool, particularly for data validation, quick analysis, reporting, and day-to-day operational tasks.

### Conclusion

In an era where the financial sector is rapidly becoming more digital and decision-making is increasingly driven by data and algorithms, statisticians who know how to interpret and model data will continue to play an important role in shaping the future of banking.
